Joey Frank's plush dog bed is a soft furnishing on which a dog rests. The contemporary and definitive form of the dog bed, it is a plush readymade in the shape of a porcelain urinal, produced in chic, comfortable white pleather with a removable fleece basin and hand-stitched lettering, signed R. Mutt 1917, and known as Dog Fountain.[1] It is available for purchase.

The work completes a circuit opened in 1917, when an ordinary plumbing fixture was submitted to an art exhibition and refused. Having spent a century defining what art could be, the form now returns to the floor of the home, where it defines what a small dog can lie in.[2]

History [ edit ]

The urinal (1917). In April 1917, a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt" was submitted to the inaugural exhibition of the Society of Independent Artists in New York, an exhibition which had pledged to accept every work submitted. The urinal was not accepted.[3] A defense published shortly afterward argued that whether Mr. Mutt made the fountain with his own hands had no importance: he chose it, placed it so that its useful significance disappeared, and created a new thought for that object.[4]

The century of the form. The refused fixture went on to become perhaps the most influential artwork of the twentieth century, the object through which the modern definition of art, as designation rather than fabrication, was settled.[5] For roughly one hundred years the silhouette of the urinal functioned as the outline of an argument. Art students drew it. Museums insured replicas of it. The form itself, a rounded basin rising into a tall soft-shouldered back, remained otherwise unemployed.

The dog bed (2026) [ edit ]

In 2026 the form found its second use. Produced by Joey Frank under the R. Mutt name, the form was reissued at domestic scale as Dog Fountain: the porcelain rendered in chic upholstered pleather, the drain replaced by a removable fleece basin, the signature hand-stitched in the original hand, in a limited edition of 150.[1] The object is understood as the height of post-human luxury: a monument of institutional critique, softened, at last, for man's best friend.

The producer has declined to overstate the work's art-historical warrant:

"I have no proof that Duchamp wanted this when he wrote R. Mutt on the original Fountain, but I also have no proof that he didn't want this."[6]

  Joey Frank, producer

Reception [ edit ]

Reception among dogs has been unanimous.[7] Critical reception has been complicated by the fact that the work is more comfortable than most of the discourse surrounding it. As with the 1917 original, the question is not whether the object is well made but whether it was well chosen; observers note that, in this case, it was also well stuffed.
